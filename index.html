The finish line moved

A young worker measures her savings against a target that keeps moving: the price of a starter home, the pay that marks a career as on track. At any moment she can stop keeping score. This page solves her problem exactly, lets you put your own answers into it, and scales it up to a whole cohort.

Part 1 · Step 3

Who should be nudged?

Below her own stopping point, this is forced persistence. Above it, it pushes her out early.

Encouragement helps someone who would quit while she is still above her stopping point. That is what a lapse looks like, and a nudge can correct it.

Encouragement hurts someone who quits at her stopping point. Every extra stretch below it is time spent feeling behind and sacrificing for a standing that is not coming back.

The same behavior, quitting, looks identical in both cases. Telling them apart means knowing where she stands and how her target moves.

How this works: each set is a ladder. The rung where you switch sides pins down one number. Coin flips with a gain and a loss measure loss aversion; money now versus later measures impatience; a raise for you alone versus a bigger raise for everyone measures how much standing matters to you. Choices about hypothetical money are noisier than choices about real money, and lab-style questions about waiting usually give higher impatience than people show over years, so treat the results as a guide, not a measurement.

“Never stops” means your savings keep close enough pace with the goal that, in the model, the comparison pays for itself at any standing.

This is not advice about whether to pursue your goal. It estimates when, for someone who chooses as you did, measuring yourself against this target stops paying for itself, and it shows what would change that: a target that rises more slowly, savings that grow faster, or a target that can swing back. Stopping the comparison is not free either: once you stop sacrificing for it, the goal usually moves further away.

Read changes, not levels. The share out of the race depends heavily on where the cohort started, which this page has to assume. How a shock or a policy moves those shares is what the model pins down.

Partial equilibrium. The benchmark is taken as given, so the lab cannot say how a cohort's disengagement feeds back into wages or prices. It links an observable benchmark to the share of a cohort that has stopped keeping score.

What this does and does not say

“Quitting” here means letting go of a comparison: no longer scoring yourself against a benchmark that moves for reasons you do not control. It is not advice about leaving work, school, treatment or a relationship, and nothing on this page tells anyone to give up a goal.

A target that jumps around makes waiting worthwhile, because it can swing back. A target that steadily pulls away makes quitting sooner the predicted response. Steadying a target cuts both ways: slowing its climb keeps people in, while damping its swings pushes those near their stopping point out.

The model counts only the cost of time spent in the race. People pushed past their limits often carry exhaustion, cynicism and lost trust long after. The true cost of forced persistence is likely larger than these numbers.
